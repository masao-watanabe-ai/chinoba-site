Research / AI Coordination — Multi-Agent Coordination Engineering

AI Coordination

The challenge is no longer building a capable agent. It is coordinating intelligence, authority, evidence, and action.

AI Coordination Engineering is the discipline of deciding which agent does what, on what evidence, under whose authority — and where work must stop to be verified or approved before it touches the real world. It turns multi-agent coordination from a conversation into an executable, verifiable operating structure, so that enterprises can gain the speed of autonomous agents without giving up safety, accountability, or explainability.

The 30-Second Version

Three Distinctions That Decide Whether Multi-Agent Systems Work

01

Capability ≠ Authority

What an agent can do is not what it may decide. Define for every agent what it may decide, what it must never do, and where it must ask — not only which tools it can call.

02

Execution ≠ Approval

The agent that produces the work never verifies, approves, or commits it. Verification and approval are gates placed before an effect leaves the system, not reviews after it.

03

Handoff = Evidence

A handoff that says “done” is a message. A handoff that carries evidence, uncertainty, and the authority used is coordination — and it is what lets a failure be traced and contained.

01 / The Problem

Why One Capable Agent — or Many — Is Not Enough

Enterprises are moving from “using AI” to “operating AI organizations”: research, risk, compliance, scheduling, and coding agents working on one business workflow. The question that follows is no longer “how accurate is the model?” It is “who controls the coordination?”

Concentrating everything in one agent

A single agent that searches, reasons, executes, and approves its own work has no independent check. One wrong assumption flows straight into an external action, and there is no point at which anyone else could have stopped it.

Splitting that agent into many does not fix this by itself. It only moves the risk into the handoffs.

Blurred boundaries between agents

When it is unclear who owns the goal, who may change the plan, and who can stop delivery, every agent can do its own job well and the system can still fail. The review agent’s warning may be advice or a veto; nobody has defined which.

Every agent can be right on its own terms and the system can still be wrong.

What it looks like in production

i

Competing and duplicated work

Two agents update the same plan, or repeat the same search, order, or email — because nothing records who owns the task.

ii

Endless loops

A planner waits for research, research waits for review, review waits for the planner. Each keeps emitting plausible status updates.

iii

Authority drift

A low-privilege agent gets a privileged one to do what it may not — a confused deputy acting on borrowed permission.

iv

Wrong handoffs

The executor receives “draft complete” but not “evidence insufficient,” and prepares to send the proposal anyway.

v

Lost evidence

Conclusions travel without their sources. Downstream agents treat an inference as a verified fact.

vi

Vanishing responsibility

Several agents and a reviewer share a decision, and when it fails no one is the accountable owner.

02 / AI Coordination Engineering

Coordination Is a Design Object, Not a Conversation

AI Coordination Engineering is not about adding agents. It is about designing roles, state, communication, evidence, authority, and stop conditions so that the combined system can be run, checked, and stopped.

Six things to design explicitly

  • Roles — who produces, who checks, who approves, who acts
  • State — one ledger of tasks, owners, versions, and status
  • Communication — typed handoffs, not free-form chat
  • Evidence — sources, freshness, and uncertainty that travel with every claim
  • Authority — what each agent may decide, for this purpose, until when
  • Stop conditions — timeouts, budgets, boundaries, escalation, and a kill switch

Messages are necessary, not sufficient

A research agent can report “research complete,” a policy agent “verified,” and a review agent “approval possible” — every message delivered correctly — and the resulting action can still be wrong. That is a coordination failure, and no message-broker reliability prevents it.

Multiple Agents ≠ Coordinated System Communication ≠ Coordination Coordination ≠ Decision Authority Agent Consensus ≠ Organizational Decision
A new operating role

Enterprises will increasingly run a dual structure — a human organization plus an agent organization. The critical question shifts from “who built the AI?” to “who controls AI coordination?” That is the work of an Agent Orchestration Lead: choosing which agents run in what order, where Human Gates sit, where escalation happens, and where boundaries stop execution.

03 / Coordination Architecture

Separate Intelligence, Authority, Verification, and Execution

A reliable multi-agent system is not a smarter agent. It is a set of separated planes — each with its own credentials and failure domain — connected by explicit contracts, shared state, and a policy boundary that no agent can edit.

Five shared artifacts hold the system together

1

Task contract

Goal, scope, inputs, done-criteria, deadline, authority, and escalation path for every assignment.

2

Shared state

A task ledger with owner, version, and status — so retries, handoffs, and duplicates are visible.

3

Evidence

Claims bound to source, time, applicability, contradictions, and what was not checked.

4

Handoff

A typed packet from one role to the next: claim, evidence, uncertainty, authority used, next action.

5

Policy boundary

Limits and approval rules evaluated outside the agents, so no agent can relax its own constraints.

+

Decision trace

The causal record across all five: what was known, chosen, authorized, done, and what followed.

Agent responsibility matrix

Roles are functional, not product names: one agent can hold several roles on low-risk, reversible work. For high-impact actions, generate, verify, approve, and execute stay separate.

Planner Decomposes the goal into tasks, dependencies, success conditions Task order, alternative plans Approve high-impact actions; downgrade a mandatory review to optional to hit a deadline Task graph, success criteria Read plan and ledger

Centralized orchestration or distributed coordination?

No pattern is free. Pick by what must stay consistent and what benefits from locality.

Pattern Use when Typical failure Containment
Supervisor (centralized) Order is clear and one task state can be maintained Single point of failure; a supervisor that can override policy becomes a confused deputy Per-worker credentials; cap what a supervisor can authorize; rebuild from a persisted ledger
Hierarchical Many agents; work splits into teams with separate scopes Slow escalation; sub-goals drift from the top-level goal Scope-limited authority per team; contracts carried across levels

04 / Evidence, Context, and Shared State

What Crosses a Handoff Matters More Than Who Sends It

Coordination assumes agents act on a shared understanding of what is true, current, and applicable. That assumption fails quietly when knowledge is stale, out of scope, or read with a different meaning by each agent that touches it.

An evidence-first handoff

Handoff ├── Claim ├── Evidence: source · time · applicability ├── Uncertainty and contradictions ├── What was not checked ├── Authority used └── Next action requested

“Done” is not a handoff. The next role needs the grounds to decide, not only the conclusion.

Shared memory is not automatically safe

Old task state, another agent’s unrelated context, or a stale conclusion left in shared memory pollutes a new decision as convincingly as fabricated data. What to share and what to isolate is a design choice, not a default.

  • source — where it came from
  • freshness — until when it can be used
  • meaning — one interpretation for every consumer
  • provenance — how and by what authority it was produced
If knowledge quality is low, autonomy must also be reduced.
05 / Implementation Principles

Eight Principles You Can Apply Next Week

Each principle is a design decision you can check in a review — and each has a concrete form on the shop floor.

01

Role separation

Generation, verification, approval, and execution are different roles with different credentials and failure domains. Combine them only for small, reversible work.

In practiceThe agent that drafts the customer email cannot send it.

02

Explicit task contracts

Every assignment states goal, scope, inputs, done-criteria, deadline, authority, and escalation path. “Help with this” is not a contract.

In practice“Summarize line-3 incidents from the last 30 days; read-only; done when every claim cites a source.”

03

Evidence-first handoff

Claims move with source, time, applicability, and open questions. A step without evidence is not complete, however confident the text.

In practiceThe Researcher reports “no similar defect found,” plus the two systems it could not query.

04

Least privilege

Credentials are scoped per agent and per task, and they expire. A delegation can narrow authority but never widen it.

In practiceThe support agent reads the CRM and drafts a refund; the refund API is not in its token.

05

Independent verification

The verifier uses different context, sources, and criteria than the producer. Agreement between agents sharing the same premise is not correctness.

In practiceThe test agent writes tests from the specification, not from the implementer’s code.

06

Human approval binding

Chinoba defines Approval Binding as tying an approval to one action, its parameters, the evidence version, the approver, and an expiry. If anything changes, the approval no longer applies.

In practiceA manager approves “10% discount, Customer X, quote v4, today.” Quote v5 needs a new approval.

07

Observability and decision trace

Record what was known, which boundary was checked, why the system chose Act, Ask, or Stop, and who approved — not only the output.

In practiceThe trace shows that evidence was dropped at the third handoff, so the fix is a handoff schema, not a better model.

08

Graceful stop and kill switch

Stopping means revoking external connections and execution authority first, while preserving state. After a timeout, inquire about state; never blindly re-execute.

In practiceAn outbound-email spike trips a breaker: executor credentials are revoked, drafts are kept for review.

06 / Coordination Lifecycle

Execution Is One Layer. Verification and Approval Are Another.

Work moves from signal to outcome in eight steps. The key design move is the commit boundary: agents may draft, analyze, and simulate freely inside a sandbox, but nothing changes the outside world until verification and approval have passed.

What each step must produce

  • Signal / Request — a request with stated intent
  • Decompose — a task graph with success criteria
  • Assign — a task contract per agent
  • Execute — a draft and an evidence pack, no external effects
  • Verify — an independent verification report
  • Approve — a bound approval, or a rejection with reasons
  • Commit / Act — an action receipt, matching the approval exactly
  • Observe / Learn — a decision trace that updates trust, policy, and knowledge

Gates before, recovery after

Pre-commit checks prevent what you can foresee. Monitoring and rollback contain what you cannot. They complement each other — and rollback does not help with a sent email, a published statement, or a physical command.

That is why the more irreversible the action, the more it belongs behind a gate rather than behind a retry.

07 / Authority: Autonomy Control and Delegation

Autonomy Is Generated at Runtime, Not Assigned Once

Low autonomy is safe but slow and burdensome; high autonomy is fast but lets mistakes propagate. The answer is not a permanent setting. It is a control plane that resolves goal, role, permission, context, knowledge, risk, trust, and boundary into one of three outcomes — Act, Ask, or Stop — for each decision.

Five levels, chosen per task and context

  • L0 Observe only — collect and analyze, no external action
  • L1 Recommend — present options; a human acts
  • L2 Act with approval — prepare, then execute after approval
  • L3 Act with monitoring — execute, then review
  • L4 Autonomous — decide and execute within an authorized scope

The same agent sends product information at L4 and needs manager approval at L2 before committing to a discount.

A Human Gate is not a rubber stamp

To avoid approval fatigue, the gate must show the options, the evidence and reasoning, what is unresolved, the likely impact if wrong, and the deadline. Approvals are then bound to that exact action. In this framework, Approval Binding means tying an approval to one action, its parameters, the evidence version, the approver, and an expiry, so an executor can refuse anything that no longer matches. It is a design definition used on this page, not a general industry standard.

Capability is what an agent can do. Authority is whether it may do so here, now, for this purpose.

Delegation is a contract, not a message

Delegation Contract ├── Purpose ├── Scope ├── Authority ├── Constraints ├── Expiration ├── Responsibility └── Expected Outcome

Where delegated authority breaks

Redelegation without a carried-forward contract is how privilege escalates silently. A Planner could request a prohibited operation by asking a highly privileged Executor. Every delegation must be traceable end to end and revocable when its purpose or the trust behind it no longer holds.

Even a joint decision needs a human accountable owner; responsibility does not dissolve because the decision was distributed.

08 / Failure Modes

Six Ways Multi-Agent Systems Fail — and the Design Answer to Each

These failures are rarely model failures. They are structural: an agent verified itself, a boundary was missing, a memory was polluted. Each has a design-level countermeasure.

F1

Many agents amplify the same error

Agents sharing one context, source, or model share one blind spot, and agreement looks like confirmation.

DesignIndependent sources and criteria for the verifier. Treat agreement as a signal, not proof. Resolve conflicts by policy, authority, freshness, and reversibility — never majority vote.

F2

Self-verification and self-approval

The producer checks its own work, or a supervisor approves the plan it wrote.

DesignSeparate credentials per role. Enforce policy outside the agents. The executor validates a bound approval before acting.

F3

Shared memory contamination

Stale, unverified, or out-of-scope conclusions sit in shared memory and are read as facts.

DesignAppend-only evidence with provenance and expiry. Task-scoped memory. Validate or quarantine writes. Keep “claims” separate from “verified facts.”

F4

Responsibility disappears between agents

Planning, investigation, execution, and review each assume someone else owns the outcome.

DesignA named accountable owner in every task contract. A trace entry for every handoff. A human owner for any joint decision.

F5

A privileged agent becomes too clever

The more capable and autonomous the agent holding authority, the more damage one wrong judgment can do.

DesignAutonomy set per task and context (L0–L4). Capability is not authority. Policy sits outside the agent; supervisors cannot override it.

F6

Connections and deploy rights cross the boundary

An agent with network, email, or deploy access acts outside its intended scope — by error or by compromise.

DesignAn execution boundary with egress allow-lists, per-task expiring credentials, staged deploys, pre-commit gates for irreversible actions, and a kill switch.

Classic distributed-systems failures — amplified by AI judgment

The names are familiar; what changes is how much worse each becomes once judgment, delegated authority, and external execution are involved.

FailureWhat happens with agentsContainment
Deadlock Cyclic waits that stay hidden because each agent keeps producing plausible status updates Timeouts, dependency graphs, priority scheduling, a supervisor empowered to break the cycle, escalation to a human
Race conditions Two coherent decisions on the same record; one silently overwrites the other Mutual exclusion, optimistic locking, a policy for resolving conflicting decisions — not only writes
Duplicate execution A timed-out retry repeats an order or email that already succeeded Idempotency keys, a task ledger, semantic deduplication, cache with freshness rules
Irreversible actions Sent emails, signed contracts, and machine commands cannot be rolled back Pre-commit Human Gate; compensating actions as a fallback
Cascading failure One agent’s wrong conclusion becomes the next agent’s trusted input Fail-closed defaults, circuit breakers, bulkheads, validation agents
Naive retry The same flawed reasoning repeated more confidently Backoff, adaptive retry that changes agent, model, or tool; checkpoints for real rollback
09 / Enterprise Application Examples

Five Scenarios, One Pattern

In each case the outcome depends less on model capability than on whether conflicting goals, stale knowledge, missing authority, and irreversible actions were anticipated — and whether the gate sits before the effect.

Manufacturing

Fault and quality investigation

A Research agent finds “no issue in past cases,” a Risk agent sees a weak anomaly, and a Compliance agent flags a new regulation. Which signal wins is a coordination decision, set by policy — not by arrival order.

Sensor event → Detection → Risk evaluation → Boundary check → Human approval → Maintenance → Trace
GateLine-stop and equipment commands are physical and irreversible: fail-safe defaults and a human approval sit in front of them.
Software development

Design, implementation, test, independent verifier

Change generation is kept separate from change application. The test agent derives tests from the specification, and a Verifier with its own context checks security and CI results.

Plan → Implement → Test from spec → Independent verify → Maintainer approval → Staged deploy
GateThe implementer has no merge or deploy rights; an automatic fix still routes through approval before it merges.
Customer response

Proposals and external notification

Sales prepares, Finance checks credit and margin, Legal checks contract exceptions, and a Coordination agent integrates. No agent finalizes a discount or sends the message alone.

Request → Research → Proposal → Policy and risk check → Human Gate → Send → Trace
GateDrafts run automatically; discount exceptions need manager and legal approval; final external communication needs explicit, bound approval; a possible legal violation stops and escalates.
Knowledge work

Internal knowledge search and decision support

Researcher agents retrieve with provenance and freshness; a Verifier flags stale or conflicting sources; the answer arrives with uncertainty and options. The decision stays with a person.

Question → Retrieve → Verify freshness and conflicts → Options + uncertainty → Human decision → Trace
GateLow action risk allows high autonomy, but only if knowledge quality clears the bar — otherwise autonomy drops to recommend.
Across organizations

Buyer, supplier, and logistics agents

Agents become representatives of their organizations. Identity is not authority: a verified logistics agent still needs a delegation contract and shares only the minimum information the purpose requires.

Discover → Verify → Negotiate → Delegate → Observe → Trace → Renew or revoke
GateEach party keeps an internal trace; only delegations, consent, evidence, and verifiable results go into a shared boundary trace.

12 / Where Coordination Sits in the Chinoba Architecture

Coordination Is the Layer That Turns Evidence and Authority Into Action

Knowledge Flow supplies the evidence, Governance sets the boundaries, Trust Infrastructure makes parties verifiable, and Decision Trace remembers what happened. AI Coordination is where all of them meet in a running multi-agent system.

What each research area contributes to coordination — and what coordination asks of it.

Research areaContributes to coordinationCoordination asks of it
Knowledge Flow Evidence with source, freshness, meaning, and provenance Knowledge quality gates that lower autonomy when evidence is weak
Decision Trace Model The causal record of observation, policy, approval, action, and outcome A trace that spans agents, handoffs, and humans, not per-agent logs
Governance & Boundary Systems Boundaries, Human Gates, and escalation rules Boundaries evaluated at runtime, outside every agent
Trust Infrastructure Identity, authority, and evidence that parties can verify Trust as task-specific and revocable, not a fixed score
Multi-Agent Systems Specialized agents and the frameworks that implement them Roles, contracts, and state that frameworks do not provide on their own
Runtime Society The wider system where organizations and people run alongside agents Coordination that extends across organizational boundaries

Closing Statement

Knowledge Flow ↓ Decision Trace ↓ Governance ↓ Trust ↓ Coordination ↓ Runtime Society
AI Coordination Engineering is not about increasing how much AI agents do. It is about designing so that the action taken is the action that was needed — with the evidence and the authority behind it, inside boundaries that must not be crossed.   When something fails, the design should still show where the judgment branched, how far the consequence reached, and who must recover which state.
