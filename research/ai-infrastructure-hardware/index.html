01 / Overview

Overview

AI infrastructure is no longer only about faster GPUs. It spans semiconductors, interconnects, memory, edge compute, and the runtime layers that connect judgment to action in the real world.

For most of its history, AI infrastructure was described in a single dimension — accelerator performance. More FLOPs, more memory, more parallelism.

That framing is no longer enough. The shape of AI workloads is changing, and with it the shape of the underlying computation.

AI infrastructure is no longer only about faster GPUs.

The next generation of AI infrastructure is composed of architectural concerns that span the full stack — from photonic-electronic integration at the silicon layer to runtime execution at the orchestration layer.

Within Scope

  • Semiconductor architecture
  • Photonic-electronic integration
  • Interposer technologies
  • Memory bandwidth
  • Edge computing
  • Distributed inference
  • Multi-agent execution infrastructure
  • Runtime-oriented hardware layers
  • Edge devices, sensors, and Physical AI
  • Trace and evidence infrastructure

The Shift

AI infrastructure used to mean racks of accelerators connected to centralized storage. It increasingly means a layered system spanning silicon, interconnect, memory, edge devices, runtime orchestration, and cross-agent coordination.

Working Statement

The next generation of AI requires not only smarter models, but new computational foundations.

This inquiry treats infrastructure as a research subject in its own right — a layered architecture that shapes which kinds of intelligence are even possible to build.

Hardware is not described here as a race of specifications. It is described as the set of components that make judgment and execution possible: what the system can observe, how quickly it can respond, and where it may act without a person in the loop.

02 / Why Infrastructure Matters

Why Infrastructure Matters

Deploying a high-performance model does not by itself produce business AI. The shift from LLMs to multi-agent and runtime-based systems changes what computation has to provide.

Traditional AI Workloads

Conventional AI infrastructure was tuned for:

  • Large model inference
  • Batch processing
  • Centralized acceleration

These workloads concentrate compute at the data center and assume that latency can be amortized over batch boundaries.

Agentic & Runtime-Based Systems

Agentic systems require something different:

  • Low-latency coordination
  • Memory-rich execution
  • Distributed processing
  • Real-time decision support
  • Edge-cloud cooperation
  • Traceable execution infrastructure
  • Energy-efficient scaling
AI infrastructure becomes part of the decision system itself.

Once agents coordinate across boundaries, the infrastructure stops being a neutral substrate and starts shaping the decision behavior.

i

Inference Speed

A multi-step agent loop of perception, action, and feedback is a continuously running system, not a single request and response.

ii

Data Movement

Context, history, and state make memory access, not arithmetic, the dominant cost. Every byte moved spends energy and time.

iii

Power & Heat

Communication between chips is far more expensive than computation inside them; power and thermal limits shape what can run where.

iv

Network Latency

Agents talk to each other and to external tools constantly. Network performance increasingly sets the performance of the whole system.

v

Device Constraints

Edge devices and machines have limited compute, memory, and energy, yet are where observations are made and actions are taken.

vi

Availability

A system that depends on external APIs, data, and context can behave differently each time. Operational AI needs a defined behavior when links fail.

vii

Operational Responsibility

When a system keeps running, someone must be able to say what it did, why, and under whose authority. Infrastructure must record this.

viii

Model ≠ Business AI

A capable model is one component. Operational AI also needs data paths, execution surfaces, controls, and traces around it.

For the LLM era, the design goal was singular — raw computational performance. A large model turned input into output, GPUs executed massive matrix operations, and the winner was whoever could deliver more FLOPS. In that world, compute was effectively everything.

That single dimension is no longer enough. GPUs alone cannot carry multi-agent, runtime-based workloads: they need CPUs for control, high-capacity low-latency memory, and high-speed low-latency networks. The limit of compute-centric design is that these pieces must now be conceived as one integrated system, not a faster accelerator bolted onto everything else.

03 / AI Runtime Stack

The AI Runtime Stack

Four layers stack from the physical world up to accountability. Two threads — Knowledge Flow and Decision Trace — run through every layer, and the whole stack is distributed across cloud, edge, and device.

1

Physical Layer

What the system is made of and touches: the machinery that computes, remembers, communicates, senses, and acts.

  • Compute
  • Memory
  • Storage
  • Network
  • Sensors
  • Actuators
2

Execution Layer

Where work is placed and kept running: across cloud, edge, and devices, coordinated and observable.

  • Cloud
  • Edge
  • Device
  • Orchestration
  • Observability
3

Intelligence Layer

What interprets and proposes: models and agents operating on knowledge that flows between them, leaving a decision trace.

  • Models
  • Agents
  • Knowledge Flow
  • Decision Trace
4

Trust Layer

What makes action legitimate: who acted, on what evidence, under which policy and authority, and when.

  • Identity
  • Evidence
  • Policy
  • Authority
  • Time
  • Accountability

Why a Stack, and Why Cross-Cutting

Lower layers constrain the upper ones: memory and network limit what an agent can hold in context and how quickly it can coordinate. Upper layers in turn govern the lower ones: policy and authority decide which actuators an agent may drive.

Two Threads Through Every Layer

Knowledge Flow carries context, state, and meaning between components. Decision Trace records when, what, and why at each step, so that execution can be reproduced, verified, and learned from.

The same stack seen from the hardware side: eight layers that together compose the AI infrastructure for multi-agent, runtime-based systems.

Distributed deployment surface — edge nodes, regional clusters, cloud back-ends — and the policies that span them.

vi

The orchestration substrate where signals, decisions, and actions are executed under constraints and timing.

vii

Negotiation, routing, escalation, and handoff between agents distributed across the infrastructure.

viii

Structured records of execution — the memory that makes infrastructure auditable, replayable, and accountable.

05 / Cloud, Edge, and Distributed Intelligence

Where Should Each Decision Run?

Which work belongs in the cloud, at a site, on a device, or next to a machine is a design question about latency, connectivity, data sensitivity, cost, autonomy, and updateability — not a question of choosing one side.

Centralized Intelligence

Large-scale models, aggregated data, training and cross-site learning, and policy authoring benefit from concentration. The cloud is where shared knowledge is built and where rules are defined once and distributed.

Situated / Distributed Intelligence

Sensing, short-loop judgment, and actuation benefit from proximity to the thing being observed. Intelligence that is situated at a site or a machine keeps working when links fail and can hold evidence close to where it was produced.

Centralized intelligence and situated intelligence are not rivals. They are complements.

06 / Hardware as a Decision Constraint

Hardware Decides How Far AI Can Act

Hardware is not only a vessel for performance. It constrains and defines when AI can look, what it can observe, and how far it can act autonomously.

From Compute Problem to System Problem

In the LLM era, AI was a compute problem: input, model, output. In the multi-agent era it is a system problem: perception, action, feedback, and loop, with state that persists and I/O that touches the outside world.

A system that keeps running acquires time and state, and with them history-dependence: behavior that is hard to explain, stop, or reproduce. Control has to be designed in, and that design reaches down to hardware.

What Conventional Architecture Lacks

A conventional AI stack of CPU, GPU, memory, storage, and network can accelerate inference and process data. From a Decision Trace Model perspective, it has no unit for deciding: no explicit place where a decision is made, constrained, and recorded.

In the LLM era, semiconductors accelerated computation. In the DTM era, they may become a foundation that executes, controls, and records decisions.
Research Hypothesis

Decision-Centric Architecture: Signal → Decision → Boundary → Execution → Trace

  1. 01 Signal Engine Interprets the situation with LLM and ML inference. Today's GPU / NPU accelerators.
  2. 02 Decision Engine Evaluates Decision Contracts with fast branching and state reference. Candidates: Arm-class CPUs, dedicated rule or DSL execution.
  3. 03 Boundary Engine Checks constraints before execution; stops or escalates. Candidates: safety controllers in the style of automotive SoCs.
  4. 04 Execution Engine Connects to external systems with high-speed, asynchronous, event-driven I/O. Candidates: CPU with DPU or SmartNIC.
  5. 05 Trace Engine Records decisions and state with low-latency, time-consistent writes. Candidates: log buffers, streaming writes, ledger-oriented storage.
A dedicated Decision Processing Unit is a research hypothesis about a possible direction for semiconductors, not an existing product category. The value of the framing is the question it asks: where, in the hardware, are decisions made, constrained, and recorded?
i

Physical AI

Sensors, actuators, and on-board compute decide which observations exist and how quickly a response can reach the physical world.

ii

Manufacturing

Field nodes at the machine, the material, and the work zone each hold a different slice of evidence; where they sit shapes what can be explained later.

iii

Robotics & Machines

Control loops near the machine need bounded, fail-safe behavior. What the hardware can guarantee defines how much autonomy can be delegated.

iv

Security & Real-Time Work

Where timing matters, the infrastructure must evaluate constraints and escalate before execution, not reconstruct what happened afterward.

A Worked Example: Field Nodes in Manufacturing

In the Chinoba PF demonstration Semantic Field Runtime Studio, three Raspberry Pi devices act as Semantic Field Nodes — one for machine condition, one for material lots and products, one for the work zone and environment. They are not treated as a small server cluster. Each is a boundary of the manufacturing environment, and each produces attributable Evidence: what was observed, when, and how.

The nodes do not infer causes. A System Twin joins their evidence into context, and agents with separated responsibilities propose hypotheses.

Keeping Inference Apart from Fact

  • Evidence — facts recorded by devices, PLCs, sensors, or readers.
  • Context — relationships resolved from time, process, and equipment.
  • Hypothesis — an AI-proposed possibility not yet verified.
  • Decision — a response chosen by a person who reviewed the grounds.
  • Outcome — the result observed after the response.

Placing evidence at the edge and judgment behind a human gate is an infrastructure choice. It is also part of the Trust Infrastructure that lets people use AI safely in operations.

07 / Communication-Centric Computing

From Compute-Centric to Communication-Centric

The center of gravity in hardware is moving away from the processor core. When many agents work together, what decides performance is no longer how fast a chip computes, but how well chips connect, share state, and coordinate.

A New Sequence

Compute-centric design optimized one thing: the speed of computation inside a chip. Communication-centric design optimizes the flow between chips — and it follows a different sequence.

Computation → Communication → Coordination

Computation still matters, but it is the easy, well-solved part. Communication — moving data and state between processors — is where power and time are now spent. And coordination — many agents aligning on shared state and intent — is the behavior all of this hardware now exists to support.

Devices That Connect

Intra-chip computation is relatively simple and efficient. Inter-chip communication is complex and far more costly — typically 10–100× the power and latency of the compute it connects. That asymmetry is what flips the design priority.

Semiconductors are evolving from "devices that compute" to "devices that connect."

The leading role of semiconductors in the coming generation is connectivity, not raw computing power. The chip becomes infrastructure for linking intelligences together, not just an engine for faster arithmetic.

The whole shift on one canvas. The processor's job moves from compute to connect to coordinate; agents surround a high-speed network fabric through which data, state, and intent flow; and the competitive edge passes from computing performance to connectivity performance. This is the picture of communication-centric computing.

Why the priority flips is a matter of cost. Computation inside a chip is cheap and well understood; communication between chips is the hidden expense, consuming on the order of 10–100× the power and time. Optimizing that communication — not adding more raw compute — is the key to next-generation performance.

The same story told as a moving bottleneck. As soon as many agents run together, the constraint slides off the compute core and onto communication, then onto shared state, then onto control. Each arrow marks where the next generation of hardware has to earn its performance.

08 / Memory & Chiplet Architecture

The Memory Wall and the Chiplet Answer

Once communication dominates cost, two hardware problems come to the front: getting data to the compute fast enough, and connecting many specialized dies without paying the full price of moving bits between them.

The Memory Wall

In agentic workloads the processor is rarely the bottleneck — feeding it is. Compute has outrun the ability of memory to supply data, so what matters is not peak FLOPS but memory bandwidth and the cost of every byte moved.

  • HBM — stacks DRAM in 3D to deliver very high bandwidth at lower power per bit.
  • Near memory — places compute beside the data to cut the distance it has to travel.
  • Data movement — the real energy and latency budget; minimizing it is the design goal.
The bottleneck is not computation. It is moving data to computation.

Why Chiplets

A single monolithic die cannot keep scaling. Chiplet architecture breaks one large chip into smaller functional dies and reconnects them at high density — trading the limits of one piece of silicon for a system of cooperating ones.

  • Chiplets — smaller functional units, improving flexibility, scalability, and cost efficiency.
  • Interposer — connects multiple dies at high density for high-bandwidth, low-latency links.
  • HBM — stacked memory placed right beside compute on the same package.
  • Optical interconnect — moves data between chips at high speed and low power using light.

Computation in Electricity, Communication in Light

On-chip computation has become steadily more efficient. Chip-to-chip communication has not: longer distances, higher drive requirements, and I/O circuitry make the energy per bit of communication far higher than that of computation. Optical links offer high speed, low power over distance, and high bandwidth, which suggests a natural division of roles.

The Interposer as an Integration Platform

An interposer is an intermediate substrate that connects multiple chips. In 2.5D packaging, logic and HBM sit millimeters apart on one interposer, linked by ultra-fine wiring and TSVs, instead of centimeters apart over a PCB. It is evolving from wiring into a platform that can also host optical I/O and chiplets made on different processes.

The new performance question is not "how fast is the core?" but "how fast can we reach the data, and how much does the trip cost?" Latency, bandwidth, and synchronization become the metrics that decide whether a fleet of agents runs as one coherent system or falls out of step.

These pressures converge on a single answer at the silicon level. Optical interconnect, chiplets, HBM, and the interposer are not four separate trends — they are one architecture, built so that communication and memory access stop being the thing that holds the system back.

09 / Distributed Intelligence

From One Large Model to an Intelligence Network

All of these hardware shifts point the same direction. AI is not heading toward a single, ever-larger model — it is heading toward many intelligences that connect, share state, and coordinate. The infrastructure is being built for that destination.

The Line of Evolution

LLM → Multi-Agent → Distributed Intelligence

A large language model is a single mind. A multi-agent system is several minds working in sequence. Distributed intelligence is a network of minds — scalable, efficient, resilient, and creative because capability lives in the connections between agents, not inside any one of them.

The AI of tomorrow is not a single model, but an intelligence network — and a network needs connective infrastructure far more than it needs a faster core.

Hardware for Coordination

This is why the semiconductor is being redefined. Its job is no longer to build the fastest possible CPU, but to connect intelligences to one another — to let agents communicate, share state, and coordinate at scale.

The chip is becoming infrastructure for connecting intelligence, not an engine for faster arithmetic.

Hardware and coordination become the same design problem: the physical layer exists to keep a distributed system of agents aligned, consistent, and able to act together.

The trajectory in one frame — from LLM to multi-agent to distributed intelligence. On the right, an intelligence network shares knowledge, state, and intent in real time, maintaining trust and adapting as the environment changes. The future of AI is built on connections of intelligence, and the hardware beneath it is being reshaped to carry them.

Made concrete: AI is no longer one giant computer but a distributed system, where agents communicate, share state, and take on roles. Collaboration, persistence, flexibility, and goal alignment are properties of the network — and each one is a demand placed directly on the interconnect, memory, and synchronization layers of the hardware.

10 / Silicon Limits & Measured Optimization

Hardware Knowledge Becomes a Search Problem

Physical limits at the silicon level push architecture toward integration, and AI itself is starting to help design and tune the hardware it runs on — through loops that are grounded in measurement.

Limits at the Silicon Level

Miniaturization, the main lever of semiconductor progress, brings increasing difficulty:

  • Leakage current and heat generation as gate dielectrics thin.
  • Short-channel effects that reduce switching control.
  • Wiring delay as thinner wires add resistance.
  • Rising manufacturing cost, quantum effects, and material limits.

Responses include new transistor structures, EUV lithography, new materials, 3D stacking, better cooling, and chiplet-style integration. AI is also being applied to EDA tools, process optimization, and, with graph neural networks, to modeling the complex interdependencies in devices and manufacturing.

LLMs as Explorers, Hardware as the Judge

Work extending K-Search to Apple Silicon, in collaboration with UC Berkeley Sky Lab, treats an LLM not as a one-shot code writer but as an explorer that forms hypotheses about bottlenecks, while measurement on the real hardware decides what is kept. Per BAIR's report, an attention kernel reached roughly 97% of Apple's native MLX kernel, and a Mamba SSM kernel reached up to roughly 20× faster prefill than the community MLX-LM implementation.

The two figures use different baselines and should not be conflated, and neither generalizes to every input shape or device. Benchmarks for multi-GPU kernels, where communication and synchronization enter, show one-shot generation remains difficult.

The relevance to infrastructure design is the loop: hypothesis, implementation, correctness check, measurement, adoption. Compile logs, tests, measurements, input conditions, and the reason for adoption play the role of a Decision Trace. People shift from writing each answer to defining workloads, evaluation criteria, permitted search space, and revalidation.
11 / Design Principles

Principles for Designing and Evaluating AI Infrastructure

A thinking frame for organizations deciding how to build and assess the foundation under their AI — independent of any particular vendor or product.

01

Workload First, Hardware Second

Start from the decisions to be supported, their timing, and their state. Choose silicon and placement afterward, and expect the bottleneck to move from compute toward communication, state, and control.

02

Data Locality

Moving data is the real cost in energy and latency. Keep processing close to the data and observations, and send out only what policy and purpose require.

03

Graceful Degradation

Define in advance what each tier does when links, devices, or models fail: continue locally, reduce scope, stop safely, or escalate to a person.

04

Observable Execution

Record when, what, and why for each step, with time-consistent evidence, so that behavior can be reproduced, verified, and improved.

05

Governed Autonomy

Make boundaries explicit before execution. Let systems act freely inside them, and return uncertain or out-of-bounds cases to a human gate.

06

Upgradeability & Interoperability

Heterogeneous parts will change at different rates. Favor modular interfaces and versioned behavior so that components can be replaced without losing the trace.
