Research / Decision Trace Model

Decision Trace Model

An AI gave an answer. Can you show why the action was allowed?

The Decision Trace Model (DTM) records a decision as one connected path — event, signal, context, decision, boundary, human gate, execution, and outcome — so that, long after the fact, you can reconstruct why an action was permitted, not only what the AI said.

  • ExplainReconstruct which signals were used, which rules and limits applied, and who approved the action.
  • ControlBegin with one high-risk decision. Add structure only where it earns its place.
  • ImproveReplay traces against outcomes to learn whether the signal, rule, boundary, or human gate should change.

01 / The Problem

Outcomes Remain. Reasons Disappear.

Most systems keep what went in, what came out, and what was updated. When a decision is questioned later, the path between those points is missing.

Signal is not Decision

AI produces signals: scores, classifications, predictions, summaries. An organization needs a decision: execute, stop, escalate, prioritize. When the relationship between the two is never defined, the same signal leads one person to execute, another to hold, and another to escalate — and nobody can later say why.

Mature records, missing reasoning

Manufacturers already manage drawings, documents, revisions, approvals, and quality traceability well. Yet it is common that no one knows why a proposal was adopted, what the alternatives were, which concerns were raised, or why it was judged acceptable at the time.

Code without history

if risk_score > 0.8: block_transaction()

Why 0.8? Who decided it? When was it changed? The decision works, but it has no history.

What this costs

  • Audit. Request, model-output, and database logs exist, yet “why was this decision made?” stays invisible.
  • Incident review. Explanations end at “at the time, there appeared to be no problem,” with no record of which concerns were accepted or set aside.
  • Improvement. You cannot tell whether the signal was inaccurate, the rule flawed, the boundary too strict, or human judgment inconsistent.
  • Accountability. You cannot tell whether the cause was the AI’s suggestion, the rule priority, the human approval, or the order of execution.
Why it matters

As AI becomes more autonomous and agentic, it can generate answers faster than organizations can decide safely. Real decisions carry ambiguity, weak signals, conflicting precedent, incomplete information, legal limits, and safety requirements.

Input → Model → Output has no place for any of this. The Decision Trace Model adds the missing decision layer between AI generation and organizational action. Its value is not a bigger archive of logs: it is being able to reconstruct the decision, the evidence it rested on, and the boundary of responsibility between AI, rules, and people.

02 / Definition

What a Decision Trace Is

A Decision Trace is the smallest record from which a decision can be reconstructed afterwards: what was observed, what was proposed, which rules and limits applied, who approved, what was done, and what happened.

Event → Signal → Decision → Boundary → Human → Log

This is the core flow of the model. Later chapters of the guides add Context before the decision, and Execution and Outcome after it.

AI generates signals; decisions are designed

A model’s output is material for judgment. The decision itself — conditions, priorities, branches, stop conditions, human involvement — is written down as structure, outside the model.

One structure for people and AI

Human and AI decisions are recorded in the same trace structure. The model began as a way to preserve human decision-making, and it does not require AI: signals can come from queries, rules, sensors, or people, with AI added later.

The five-question test

A trace is complete enough when it can answer:

  1. What event occurred?
  2. Which model generated the signal?
  3. Which rule determined the decision?
  4. Which boundary was applied?
  5. Who approved the final outcome?
Research direction

DTM 2.0 asks for the “why” behind the decision. It places Need, Goal, Constraint, Context, and Lack before the event, adds Interpretation between signal and decision, and closes the loop through Outcome, Feedback, Learning, and Knowledge. The same signal can mean different things depending on what is being pursued and what is missing.

The expanded structure may be better named a Meaning Trace. It connects this line of work to Runtime Society and Trust Infrastructure.

03 / Structure

Anatomy of a Trace

Nine elements, using the vocabulary of the guides. Each answers a question you will be asked after the fact.

01

Event

Records What happened and what triggered the decision: an inquiry, an anomaly alert, an approval request.

Why Decisions never start from nothing. The event gives the trace its context.

02

Signal

Records What AI or agents proposed: predictions, scores, classifications, summaries, candidate actions, including those not adopted.

Why Shows which outputs were used and which were set aside. A signal is input to a decision, never the decision.

03

Context

Records History, similar past cases, current state, and human knowledge, including where they conflict.

Why The same signal means different things in different contexts. Conflicting evidence is itself a risk signal.

04

Decision

Records Which decision contract, rules, and priorities were applied; what was selected and what was not.

Why A bare “approved” cannot be reviewed. A structure of selection can.

05

Boundary

Records The limits and policies checked (regulatory, budget, safety, risk) and the result: act, ask, or stop.

Why Explains why something was not executed, and which constraint stopped it.

06

Human Gate

Records Who approved, rejected, or allowed an exception; under what authority; what the reviewer weighed.

Why Without it, human-in-the-loop is an opaque black box of responsibility.

07

Execution

Records The action actually taken, by which system, agent, or person, tied to the decision that authorized it.

Why A decision that cannot be tied to an action cannot be verified.

08

Outcome

Records What happened afterwards, attached later to the same trace: sales, waste, a follow-up complaint, a failure avoided.

Why Lets you judge the decision, not only the model. This is where the loop closes.

09

Ledger

Records All of the above as an ordered, append-only trace under one trace_id, hash-chained with prev_hash and event_hash.

Why History cannot be quietly rewritten, it can be replayed, and improvements are recorded as new traces.

The five primitives behind the nine elements

The model was first described with five primitives. They are the same structure seen from the runtime side: each one maps onto the elements above.

i

Signal

AI-generated outputs: predictions, anomalies, recommendations, text. Informative, not authoritative.

Trace Event, Signal

ii

Runtime

The orchestration layer that evaluates signals, forms context, checks boundaries, coordinates agents, and records the trace.

Trace Context, Decision, Execution

iii

Boundary

Safety limits, escalation rules, operational constraints, and governance conditions that stop unsafe automatic execution.

Trace Boundary

iv

Human Gate

Critical decisions require explicit human involvement. Human-in-the-loop is architectural, not optional.

Trace Human Gate

v

Decision Trace

Every important decision stays traceable, explainable, auditable, and reusable, forming organizational memory.

Trace Outcome, Ledger

The guides’ ledger example also stores a policy name and version next to the boundary (for instance fraud_policy_v2). Chinoba’s Knowledge Flow research adds the Evidence Pack and Retrieval Receipt as the records of what a decision relied on.

04 / Lifecycle

From Signal to Review

A decision is not a moment. It is a path with checkpoints, and the trace lets you walk it backward for audit and forward for improvement.

A human gate is a designed node, not a button

“A human reviews if needed” leaves unclear when people should step in and what they are responsible for. A designed gate states when to escalate, what the person decides, what authority they hold, and how far an override may go.

The reviewer is not rubber-stamping AI output. In the manufacturing case below, they weigh signal strength, similarity to past failures, counterexamples, safety impact, causal uncertainty, validation cost, and the risk of misjudgment, and may reach an intermediate decision such as HOLD_WITH_ADDITIONAL_VALIDATION.

Approval Binding

Approving a summary is not approving an action. Approval Binding ties what the person reviewed to what is executed — for example by hash — and sends the case back for reapproval if the content, recipient, or policy changes. It is one of five implementation building blocks in Chinoba’s Philosophy of AI research. The DTM guides do not define it; it is the natural partner of the Human Gate.

Why the record resists tampering and substitution

  • Append-only. Events are never overwritten or deleted. Improvements are recorded as new traces; the past is not rewritten.
  • One trace per decision. A shared trace_id and event order keep causality, not just time order, so the path can be replayed.
  • Hash chain. Each event references the hash of the previous one, so tampering or a missing event is detectable. No full blockchain is required.
  • Replay. Re-run a past decision under the same conditions, or with a different rule or boundary, to compare results.

Stopping is a property of the decision

Telling an agent “stop if it is dangerous” in a prompt is not a structure. The guides place a boundary after the decision, with three kinds of stop: a hard stop for critical risk, a soft stop that hands the case to a person, and a redirect to another route. Safety is layered: permission control, decision and boundary, human responsibility, and the log.

05 / Use Cases

Three Decisions, Traced

Each case comes from the guides and articles behind this page. For each: what is missing without a trace, what the trace holds, and what you can check afterwards.

Manufacturing · Quality judgment

An ambiguous signal in a safety-related design

Voltage becomes slightly unstable at high temperature. The threshold is not exceeded, similar cases exist, and their outcomes differed. It may be a real failure, or noise.

Before: what is missing

  • Design reviews lean on veteran experience, memory of past incidents, departmental power balance, and the mood of the meeting.
  • A simple RAG answer stops at “there are similar cases.” It cannot handle contradictions between cases, safety boundaries, or escalation.
  • Why the decision was made is poorly preserved.

Decision Trace: what is recorded

  • The weak signal is kept (risk score 0.38), not dismissed as “within threshold.”
  • Past failure traces, QA notes, and the ledger are searched. Two cases conflict, and the conflict raises the risk score to 0.52.
  • Two knowledge holders are consulted: QA thermal validation (similarity 0.84, risk 0.68) and field failure analysis (the 2024 case was sensor noise).
  • Boundary escalation: safety-related ambiguity, conflicting precedent, insufficient causal certainty. Status: WAITING_HUMAN_REVIEW.
  • Decision: HOLD_WITH_ADDITIONAL_VALIDATION, with a long-duration thermal test, sensor noise isolation, and extra QA validation required.

After: what you can verify

  • Why the release was held rather than approved or rejected.
  • Which cases and which people informed the call.
  • That the conflict itself triggered escalation.
  • Later signals about thermal instability can reference how the organization decided, not only what documents say.
Source: scenario in the DTM article on decision ambiguity at the boundary. The scores belong to that illustrative scenario.
Maintenance · High-risk autonomous action

A maintenance-support AI near safety-critical equipment

The AI reads sensor data, maintenance records, inventory, failure history, and worker qualifications, proposes inspection plans, and may issue work orders for minor inspections.

Before: what is missing

  • Bug-free code is necessary but not sufficient. The open questions are about authority and boundaries.
  • May the AI decide that safety-critical equipment can keep running without human approval? What if a sensor value is missing?
  • After an accident, can the data, rules, responsible parties, and exceptions be reconstructed?

Decision Trace: what is recorded

  • The Decision Law checked before execution: continuing safety-critical equipment needs a qualified human’s approval; a missing required sensor value or inspection record blocks an automatic “safe” finding.
  • Automatic parts procurement only when approved budget, supplier, and spending limit are all met.
  • Any proposal to avoid a shutdown carries alternatives, a risk assessment, and evidence references.
  • The result as Act, Ask, or Stop, with its reason. If inputs, rules, evidence, executor, and outcome cannot be retained, the action is not executed.

After: what you can verify

  • That evidence, authority, and boundaries were satisfied before execution, not argued afterwards.
  • Who approved, and under what authority.
  • Which laws were missing or too coarse: exceptions, Human Gate rejections, and near misses accumulate as traces and show where the laws need revising.
Source: the article on verifiable autonomy and Decision Law. Autonomy is not binary: Act, Ask, and Stop are decided against laws written in advance, not by model confidence alone.
Customer support · Replay and improvement

An automatic reply that became a complaint

An inquiry is classified as an FAQ and answered automatically. Later it escalates into a complaint.

Before: what is missing

  • The log holds the inquiry text, the classification, the reply, and the completion time.
  • It does not explain why an automatic reply was sent, or why a similar case was escalated before.

Decision Trace: what is recorded

  • Event: inquiry received.
  • Signal: LLM classification FAQ, confidence 0.82.
  • Decision: confidence ≥ 0.80, therefore auto_reply.
  • Boundary: no issue, the customer is not a VIP. Human: no intervention.
  • Action: automatic reply. Outcome: later escalated into a complaint.

After: what you can verify

  • Replay the decision and ask whether the 0.80 threshold was too low.
  • Ask whether checking sentiment would have prevented it, even for an FAQ.
  • Ask whether complaint history belongs in the boundary for non-VIP customers.
  • The target of improvement is the decision, not only model accuracy.
Source: the Decision Trace Ledger chapter of the practical guide.

The same sources also trace retail store decisions (option, concern, rejected option, decision, outcome), finance (approve, reject, modify, hold), software development, education, and UX. Retail is the one the guide suggests starting by hand: structure roughly 200–500 store decisions before adding search or AI assistance.

06 / Implementation

Implementation Guide

Start with the smallest trace that can answer an auditor’s question, on the decision where a wrong answer is hardest to undo. Do not begin with a complete runtime: a minimal one is still a runtime.

Fit it into what you already run

A trace layer sits beside existing systems. It connects information you already have rather than replacing it.

Logging

Existing log stack

Keep your logs. Add a trace_id so request, model-output, and workflow events about one decision can be joined. The ledger core stays minimal; databases remain external.

RAG and search

Store the decision, not just the document

RAG retrieves documents for a model to summarize. A trace store lets you also retrieve how past cases were decided: why one was escalated, why another was approved.

Evidence

Evidence and receipts

Link each signal and context item to its source through an Evidence Pack and Retrieval Receipt, as described in Knowledge Flow.

Workflow

Approvals as human gates

Model each approval as an explicit node with authority and override scope, and bind the approval to the action that runs.

Orchestration

Orchestrator writes the trace

An orchestrator runs the decision flow (for example as a behavior tree) and writes each step, including boundary and human gate results, to the ledger.

Audit

Audit and replay

With an append-only ledger, auditors can ask which model, which rule, which boundary, and who approved — and replay the decision under changed rules.

Start where a wrong decision is hardest to undo

Most organizations already hold pieces of this: quality events, approvals, review processes. The first step is tracing existing decisions, not inventing new ones. AI can come later.

  1. Step 1

    Pick one decision

    Choose one high-risk process. Interview experienced staff, collect typical and exceptional cases, and write down Event, Signal, Decision, Boundary, and Human, with priorities such as Safety > Quality > Cost.

  2. Step 2

    Separate signal from decision

    Keep your existing models. Put a small decision layer behind them with minimal rules and at least one human point. Do not push the rule back into the LLM prompt.

  3. Step 3

    Record the trace

    Capture the minimum fields under a trace_id. Always record human intervention. Attach outcomes after a set period. A CSV or database is enough; never delete history.

  4. Step 4

    Review and improve

    Review representative traces. Sort each finding into model, rule, boundary, or human gate. Change one thing and run the cycle again.

  5. Step 5

    Expand when needed

    Add a rule language, an independent boundary, behavior trees, the hash-chained ledger, or multiple agents only where the earlier steps show a limit.

07 / Design Principles

Principles and Anti-Patterns

Four principles from the guides, and seven failure patterns to design against, each with a short fix.

Principle 1

Separate signal and decision

AI does not decide. Decisions are externalized as rules, so changing a model does not break the decision.

Principle 2

Connect decision to execution

Every decision is translated into an action, and the action is recorded against it.

Principle 3

Define the boundary first

Decide what must never be done, where the system stops, and what returns to humans, before deciding what to automate.

Principle 4

Close the loop through the log

Decision, result, improvement. The ledger serves improvement as well as audit.

Anti-patterns and fixes
Anti-patternWhat goes wrongFix
No evidence link The decision cannot be checked against what it relied on. Explanations become recollection. Record references to the signals, sources, and people used. Keep conflicts and rejected options. Link to the Evidence Pack where you have one.
Decision and execution not tied together A decision exists, but nobody can show it was the one that ran. Put every step under one trace_id, in order, from event to outcome, and record the action against its decision.
Approved object differs from executed object A person approved a summary, and something else ran. Apply Approval Binding: bind the reviewed content to the executed action, for example by hash, and return for reapproval on any change.
Policy, model, and prompt versions not kept Replay answers with today’s rules and model, not the ones in force at the time. Record the policy version and the producing model with each signal. Keep decision rules outside the prompt. Replay from a snapshot fixed at decision time.
Blurred responsibility among people, AI, and rules No one can say whether the suggestion, the rule priority, the approval, or the execution order caused the outcome. Agents emit signals. A decision layer applies the contract. The boundary can stop. The human gate is a named node with authority and override scope.
Raw logs, but no searchable decisions Reasons can only be inferred from fragments. Make the decision the unit of storage. Index traces by case and retrieve past decisions alongside documents.
Safety by instruction “Stop if dangerous” in a prompt is not enforced. An agent generates the action that seems right at the moment. Enforce stops at the boundary: hard stop, soft stop to a human, or redirect. The agent does not stop; the decision does.

Summary

From Logging Outputs to Operating Decisions

The shift is small to state and large in effect: stop treating the AI’s output as the thing to record, and start treating the decision as an asset to keep, replay, and improve.

From

Record the output

What went in, what came out, what was updated. Results survive; reasons do not.

To

Record the path to the action

Signals, context, rules, boundaries, approvals, execution, and outcome as one ordered trace.

Then

Operate it as an asset

Replay, review, and revise signals, rules, boundaries, and human gates. Knowledge becomes decision memory.
