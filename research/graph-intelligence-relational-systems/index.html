Research / Graph Intelligence & Relational Systems

Graph Intelligence: From Connected Information to Evidence-Grounded Decisions

関係性から学び、根拠ある判断へつなぐ。

Intelligence does not live only in isolated records. It is shaped by relationships, and by the boundaries people draw around them.

This inquiry studies how relationships among people, organizations, knowledge, machines and AI agents can be represented, learned from and governed. Graph neural networks are its technical foundation; evidence-grounded retrieval and Decision Trace are applied architectures for enterprise use; and human-set boundaries decide what any of it may do.

01 / Why Relations

Intelligence Is Shaped by the Edges

The gap is rarely missing data. It is missing relations: among information, people, work, decisions and evidence.

Information exists. Its relations are cut.

Most organizations are not short of information. Specifications, notes, chat threads, equipment logs, customer histories, rules and past decisions all exist — in different systems, owned by different people, written at different times. What is missing is the link between them: which spec a defect report concerns, which rule applied on that date, who approved the exception, and why.

Searching returns passages that resemble a question. Deciding needs to know how things bear on one another.

Value sits in connections, not in points

Customer, HR and sales systems record what exists. They rarely record how things are connected. Two customers with the same attributes can behave differently because their relationships differ — which communities they belong to, who influences them, which services they trust.

Intelligence is not stored in a node. It is shaped by the edges.

The same holds inside an organization, and increasingly between people and AI agents. Graph Intelligence is the study of how such relationships can be represented, learned from and reasoned over.

02 / Graph Learning Foundations

Graph Neural Networks: Learning From Relational Structure

The technical base of this inquiry — how models learn from entities and the relationships between them.

A graph neural network (GNN) is a neural network for data whose elements are connected: social networks, road networks, molecules, knowledge graphs, production lines. It is the technical foundation of this inquiry — the part that learns from relational structure.

i

Representation learning

Nodes (and edges) are mapped to low-dimensional vectors that preserve graph structure, so downstream models can use relationships as input.

ii

Message passing

Each node updates its state from its neighbors, layer by layer. Structure therefore shapes what the model represents.

iii

Architectures

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNN) are common families.

iv

Tasks

Node classification, link prediction, graph classification and graph generation.

v

Learning modes

Semi-supervised and unsupervised learning; combinable with CNNs and RNNs when the data also has sequence or image structure.

vi

What is learned

Statistics of relationships — for example that a structure like A → B → C recurs. Not why, and not where a decision should stop (see §07).

The same primitive — entities as nodes, relationships as edges — appears across the domains covered in the research notes.

03 / Dynamic & Multi-Agent

From Static Graphs to Change and Coordination

Relations change over time, can be altered hypothetically, and, once agents act, become a coordination problem.

i

Dynamic graphs

Nodes and edges change over time. Dynamic GNNs model temporal dependencies and time-varying connections, often combined with RNN or LSTM components. Typical uses: traffic forecasting, social and biological networks, financial data.

ii

Counterfactual learning

Ask “what if this node had a different attribute, or this edge did not exist?” Build the altered graph, train on both, and compare outcomes. This links graph learning to causal reasoning — as hypotheses, not verdicts.

iii

Multi-agent graphs

Agents are nodes; communication and dependency are edges. Each agent integrates its own state with its neighbors’ to choose actions, and the system learns from rewards. Communication and cooperation become part of the model.

iv

Live process graphs

In manufacturing, machines, operations and operators form a graph that is first designed and simulated, then updated from real-time data to support optimization and early anomaly detection.

Moving from static to dynamic and multi-agent graphs changes the question from “what is connected?” to “what is connected now, and wha happens if it changes?” It also raises the stakes: once agents act on the graph, someone must decide what they are permitted to do (§07).

04 / Community Graph & Relationship Capital

Relationships as Assets — and AI as an Accountable Actor

Extending the graph from data to society: people, organizations, knowledge and AI agents, with explicit authority and accountability.

A graph of who is connected to what

A Community Graph represents the actors of a community — people, organizations, knowledge, services and now AI — and the relationships among them. It is not a directory. It records who is connected to what, and in what meaningful way, including how one person plays several roles (parent, manager, resident, customer) as a single node.

Inside a company it works as a dynamic map of how work actually flows: who was involved in a decision, which knowledge was used, where information stopped, which departmental links are weak.

Relationships have histories

A link is not complete once created. It changes. A useful graph records when a relationship began, how strong it is, whether it is still active, which events changed it, and which decisions or actions it influenced. Relationships become evolving assets rather than static rows.

Relationship Capital

Facilities, patents and data are assets. So are ongoing customer relationships, collaboration between companies, trust in a local community, shared work among employees, and human–AI collaboration. These rarely appear on a balance sheet but shape future value: companies using the same AI can differ because of the relationships around it.

AI as an actor, not only a tool

As agents spread, AI takes on roles: supporting sales, reviewing contracts, watching equipment, answering inquiries, coordinating other agents. Each has different knowledge, authority and area of responsibility. In a Community Graph an AI is a node like any other, and the graph can state who uses it, which organization it belongs to, what knowledge it may access, which decisions it may be entrusted with, which agents it works with — and who holds final accountability.

Trust in AI is not trust between people

It is built from observable properties:

  • accuracy and consistency
  • explainability
  • clear authority boundaries
  • privacy protection
  • human control
  • traceable decision histories

The graph can hold edges such as uses, delegates to, supervises, approves and sets a trust level for. The question it answers is not only what AI can do, but in whose relationship, and to what extent, it can be entrusted.

06 / Applied Architecture

Graph RAG, Evidence Graph and Decision Trace

How relational representation can be carried into enterprise knowledge and decision systems — an application layer built on the foundations above.

Scope note. Graph RAG, Evidence Graph, Semantic Chunk, Retrieval Receipt and Decision Trace are not described in the research notes, and the notes do not lead to them directly. They are Chinoba’s applied architecture: one way to carry relational learning and relational representation into enterprise knowledge and decision systems, with evidence and human approval kept in the loop.

Vectors and graphs complement each other

Vector search is fast, flexible and needs little modeling. It finds passages that resemble a question and normally stays as the firs step. It does not hold who is responsible, which version was in force, what an exception depends on or which approval path applies. Those are properties of relations.

Graphs are not a universal remedy

They cost modeling effort, inherit extraction errors and need upkeep. The comparison below describes typical designs, no product capabilities; a well-built RAG pipeline can add metadata and versions, and a graph can be built badly.

From source to Decision Trace

Every step leaves something behind: provenance at the source, evidence on every edge, a receipt at retrieval, an approver at execution, a trace afterward. The graph is the middle of the chain, not the whole of it.

i

Relational retrieval

Questions that span entities: which open orders use parts from the lot flagged last week?

ii

Evidence tracing

Every answer can be walked back to relations and source passages, including the version used.

iii

State, time & constraints

What was valid when, and which limits applied — checkable against the state at decision time.

iv

Exceptions & contradictions

Conflicting sources, orphaned approvals and rules without an owner appear as structure.

v

Shared agent context

Agents read the same entities, evidence and constraints instead of rebuilding context from prompts.

vi

Improvement via trace

Past decisions with their evidence and outcomes become material for reviewing future ones.

The Decision Trace is also where this architecture meets graph learning: a ledger records the decision process, a graph connects the judgments, a GNN learns relational patterns, and past judgments suppor later ones. This is Chinoba’s Decision Trace GNN proposal — a research direction, not a settled method.

07 / Boundaries, Stop Conditions & Authority

Graphs Learn Relations. Design Sets the Boundaries.

What a GNN or an agent understands is separate from what it is permitted to do. The line between them is written by people.

Meaning appears where a line is drawn

Approximation treats 0.49 and 0.51 almost alike. A decision does not: at some point the difference becomes pass or fail, allowed or denied, safe or dangerous. That jump lies outside smooth approximation, and the better a model approximates, the more such boundaries blur — which is why an answer can be fluent and still feel wrong.

A GNN comes closer to meaning than most models, because meaning often lives in relationships: a design may be rejected because of a component constraint, a safety standard, a past accident, cost. But what a GNN learns is the statistics of relationships.

Design writes the discontinuities

A GNN can learn that A → B → C recurs. It cannot decide that the decision should stop here. That is a designed rule.

Boundaries, stop conditions and exception handling are where meaning remains:

  • Do not decide below this probability.
  • Do not automate this type of case.
  • Return this case to human judgment.

The question is not whether AI can understand meaning, but who writes the discontinuities, and where they are written.

Layer Diagram

Three layers of AI

  1. 1 Continuous approximationLLMs, deep learning, transformers: smooth function approximation in vector space. approximates the world
  2. ↓
  3. 2 Relational structureGraph neural networks: learn which things connect and what patterns recur. learns relationships
  4. ↓
  5. 3 Semantic discontinuityOntology, rules, boundaries: pass / fail, allowed / denied, stop / continue. designed by people

Understanding is not authority

A graph can make an agent very good at seeing how things are connected. That says nothing about what it may do. Capability — reading, inferring, proposing — and authority — the right to change something in the world — are granted, held and revoked separately. Where agents are nodes in a Community Graph (§04), their authority edges are set by people, not learned.

The intelligence that understands relations must not be the authority that permits execution.

Design principles

  • Authority is granted by a control layer outside the model and the graph.
  • An approval binds to the exact action and the evidence version it relied on.
  • Approvals expire and are consumed; they are not reusable.
  • The verifier is independent of the executor.
  • Missing evidence, contradiction or low confidence escalates to a person.
  • Every proposal, approval and outcome goes into the Decision Trace.

Limits to state plainly

  • Graph quality is bounded by extraction quality; a wrong edge can look authoritative.
  • LLMs can invent relations or misread evidence, so extracted edges need confidence and review.
  • GNN predictions are statistical; they do not set boundaries, exceptions or permissions.
  • Agents act on what they are given. Responsibility for the result stays with named people.

08 / Roadmap

Research Directions and Application Maturity

Open questions for the inquiry, and four cumulative levels for organizations adopting the applied architecture.

Research directions

Open questions this inquiry intends to pursue. They are directions, not commitments or results.

i

Decision Trace GNN

Learning relational patterns from ledgered decisions, with each trace tied to its boundary.

ii

Dynamic evidence graphs

Connecting temporal graph learning with validity periods and supersession in the Evidence Graph.

iii

Counterfactual change impact

Using “what if this edge were removed” as reviewed hypotheses for engineering and policy changes.

iv

Authority-aware Community Graphs

Modeling delegation, supervision and accountability edges for AI actors, set by people.

v

Boundary-aware evaluation

Testing whether systems escalate when evidence is missing or contradictory, rather than only measuring accuracy.

vi

Relationship Capital

Finding workable ways to observe the strength and history of relationships without turning people into scores.

Application maturity

For organizations adopting the applied architecture of §06, four cumulative levels. Most value at L1–L3 comes from data hygiene and review habits, not model size; do not move to L4 before L2–L3 controls work.

L1

Connected Retrieval

Data: documents, master data, entity links.
Operations: entity resolution; link curation.
Governance: source access rights carried over; read-only.

L2

Evidence-Grounded Answers

Data: + Semantic Chunks with provenance and versions.
Operations: citation checks; retrieval receipts; evaluation sets.
Governance: every answer cites sources; unsupported answers are declined.

L3

Decision Support

Data: + constraints, state, past decisions.
Operations: reviewers inside the workflow; option comparison.
Governance: named decision owner; a Decision Trace per proposal; escalation rules.

L4

Governed Agent Coordination

Data: + roles, authority, agent identities.
Operations: orchestration; independent verifier; revocation and stop controls.
Governance: scoped authority; approval binding; audit and periodic review.
