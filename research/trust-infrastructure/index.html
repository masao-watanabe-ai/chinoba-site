Research / Trust Infrastructure

Trust Infrastructure for AI Systems

From AI output to accountable action.

An AI system that answers well is not yet one an organization can safely let act. Once an agent can send a customer notice, change a permission, deploy code, or release a payment, the question moves from “is the answer correct?” to “on what evidence, under whose authority, and with what proof that what ran is what a person approved?” Trust Infrastructure is the implementable foundation that connects evidence, authority, approval, execution, and audit, so people and organizations can rely on AI for consequential work.

Trust is not added after an AI system acts. It must be designed into the path from evidence to execution.

01 / The Problem

When AI Moves From Answering to Acting

A chat assistant that is wrong produces a bad answer. An agent connected to a payment system, an identity directory, a deployment pipeline, or a customer channel can produce a bad action — and some actions are hard to undo. Accuracy still matters, but it stops being the only question.

What Changes Once Agents Are Connected to Tools

  • Payments and procurement: a proposal becomes a released payment or a placed order
  • Identity and permissions: a request becomes a granted or revoked access right
  • Customer communication: a draft becomes a message in a customer's inbox
  • Deployment and infrastructure: a plan becomes a change in production
  • Workflows: one agent's output triggers the next system's action

A Fluent Answer Is Not a Control

Models can sound equally confident when they are right and when they are wrong. Review by a person helps only if the person sees the right things and the system then does exactly what they approved. Without that, “human in the loop” can become a click that carries no verifiable meaning.

Five Questions

What an organization must be able to answer before an AI acts

  1. Q1 What evidence supports this action? Sources, versions, and freshness — not only a fluent rationale. evidence
  2. Q2 Who is allowed to authorize it? Authority belongs to a role, a scope, and a time window — not to whichever model is most capable. authority
  3. Q3 What exactly did the human approve? A summary on a screen is not the same thing as the action payload that will run. approval
  4. Q4 Can the executed action be proven to match the approved action? Between approval and execution, a target, an amount, or a message can change. execution
  5. Q5 Can the decision be reconstructed later? Audit, dispute, and learning all need the whole chain, not just the outcome. trace

Making the model smarter does not, by itself, answer any of these. They are design questions about the system around the model — which is why this page starts with how AI is allowed to act, and only then with how well it reasons.

02 / Definition

What Trust Infrastructure Is

Trust Infrastructure is the set of connected mechanisms that carry an AI-assisted decision from evidence to execution and back into the record. An audit log is one part of it and an identity system is another. The value lies in the connections between them.

i

Evidence

What a proposal rests on: sources, versions, provenance, and freshness.

ii

Identity

Who or what is acting — user, agent, or service — each distinguishable and attributable.

iii

Authority

What that identity may do, in which scope and for how long, and who can delegate or revoke it.

iv

Policy

Rules written so a system can enforce them, not only so a person can read them.

v

Approval Binding

A human decision tied to the exact action payload, not to a summary of it.

vi

Execution Boundary

The point where intent becomes effect: credentials are held here and checks are run again here.

vii

Decision Trace

The structured record linking evidence, options, authority, approval, action, and result.

viii

Auditability

The ability to reconstruct and examine a past decision for review, dispute, and improvement.

More Than Logs or Login

Logging records what happened; it does not decide what may happen. Authentication establishes who is acting; it does not say whether this action is within scope. A governance document states intent; it does not stop an action. Trust Infrastructure turns each of these into an enforced step on one path.

In Chinoba's research architecture, the Decision Trace Model supplies the record, Knowledge Flow supplies the evidence, and Governance & Boundary Systems supply the limits. This page covers how they are wired into a single executable path.

How the Pieces Relate

Evidence → Proposal (AI Agent) → Policy and Authority check → Human approval, bound to the payload → Execution Boundary → Decision Trace → Audit and learning

Each step produces something the next step can verify. If a step cannot be verified, the path stops rather than guessing.

03 / Core Principles

Seven Design Principles

These are properties of the system around the model. They are meant to hold even when the model is wrong, manipulated, or simply unlucky.

1

Evidence before action

A proposal carries its sources, versions, and freshness. Missing or stale evidence is a reason to hold, not to guess.

2

Authority is explicit and scoped

Each agent and role has defined actions, resources, limits, and duration. Capability is never treated as permission.

3

Approval is bound to an immutable action payload

What a person approves is the exact, fixed payload. Any change to it invalidates the approval.

4

High-risk execution is separated from intelligence

The component that reasons does not hold the credentials. A restricted gateway executes.

5

Policies are enforceable, not merely documented

Rules live in a form a deterministic layer can evaluate, with versions, owners, and effective dates.

6

Every consequential decision leaves a reconstructable trace

Evidence, options, authority, approval, action, and result are linked so the decision can be examined later.

7

Revocation, expiry, and emergency stop are designed in advance

Grants expire by default and can be withdrawn at once. A tested stop path exists before it is needed.

=

Taken together

Trust is built into the path from evidence to execution, rather than added once an action has already happened.

04 / Practical Architecture

Models Propose. Deterministic Layers Decide What Runs.

The central design decision is a separation. A model may reason and propose. Deterministic layers decide what is allowed, verify what was approved, control execution, and write the record.

Roles

Who does what

  1. 01 Knowledge / Evidence Layer Retrieves sources with version, owner, validity period, and scope, so the agent receives evidence and not only text. evidence
  2. 02 AI Agent Interprets the situation, drafts, summarizes, and generates candidate actions. Its output is a proposal. probabilistic
  3. 03 Policy Engine Evaluates identity, role, scope, risk tier, and rules against the proposal, and returns allow, require approval, hold, or deny. deterministic
  4. 04 Human Approval Presents the evidence, the exact payload, the risk, and the deadline, and records approve, reject, or modify against that payload. bound
  5. 05 Execution Gateway Holds the credentials. Verifies the approval, re-validates context, executes once, and fails closed if anything cannot be verified. deterministic
  6. 06 Decision Trace / Audit Records the chain in a tamper-evident, append-only form for reconstruction, review, and learning. record

What a Model Can Do

  • Summarize evidence for the reviewer
  • Draft a message, a plan, or a change
  • Generate and rank candidate actions
  • Explain its rationale in reviewable terms

These are valuable, and they are where model capability belongs.

What Must Stay Deterministic

  • Authority decisions: who may do what, within which scope
  • Approval verification: does this execution match what was approved?
  • Execution control: credentials, limits, and fail-closed behavior
  • The audit record itself
Smartness must not become authority.
05 / Approval Binding

What the Human Saw Must Be What Runs

An approval is meaningful only if it refers to the exact thing that later happens. Approval Binding ties a human decision to a fixed action payload in a way that detects any later change.

Why a Click Is Not Enough

A reviewer approves: “send Customer A the renewal offer on terms X, with message Y.” If the recipient, the terms, or the text change before execution — through a bug, a prompt injection, a retry, or a later edit — the approval no longer describes what happens. The system has to notice, not assume.

Approval Binding is therefore not extra bureaucracy. It is what gives a human approval a checkable meaning.

How Binding Works, in Plain Terms

  1. The proposed action is frozen as a payload: target, parameters, text, and scope.
  2. A fingerprint (hash) of that payload is computed. Any change to the payload changes the fingerprint.
  3. The reviewer sees the payload and its evidence, and approves or rejects exactly that.
  4. The approval is recorded with the fingerprint, the approver, the scope, and an expiry time, and is signed so it cannot be altered unnoticed.
  5. At execution, the gateway recomputes the fingerprint, confirms it matches, confirms the approval is unexpired and unused, and checks current policy and context again.
i

Approval payload

The frozen description of the action: who, what, how much, which message, which system.

ii

Hash

A fingerprint of the payload. If a single character changes, the fingerprint no longer matches.

iii

Scope

The limits of the approval: this customer, this amount ceiling, this system, this set of changes.

iv

Expiry

Approvals age out. Prices, context, and policy move, and a stale approval should not execute.

v

Single-use token

Each approval authorizes one execution. A retry or a replay finds it already spent.

vi

Re-validation

At the moment of execution, policy, authority, and context are checked again rather than trusted from earlier.

For actions that cannot be undone, separate prepare from commit: first lock the target and fix the plan and its expected impact, then re-verify policy, context, approval, and expiry immediately before executing. If the context has changed, the decision is made again.

06 / Concrete Use Cases

Five Places the Pattern Applies

The same five-part shape recurs: the AI proposes, constraints bound the proposal, a person approves the exact action, an execution boundary enforces that approval, and a record remains. The details below are illustrative designs, not claims about any deployed system.

Customer communication

Customer Notification Agent

AI proposes
Drafts a notice for an affected customer segment, with selected terms.
Constraints
Approved templates only; no unreleased commitments; recipient list taken from a verified source; consent and timing rules applied.
Human approval
The account owner approves the exact recipient set, terms, and message text.
Execution boundary
The messaging gateway sends only the approved payload; a changed recipient or text is rejected.
Record kept
Evidence used, template version, approved payload hash, send time, and delivery result.
Engineering

Deployment and Infrastructure Change Agent

AI proposes
Proposes a change plan and a configuration diff for a service.
Constraints
Allowed environments and change windows; blast-radius limits; required tests passed.
Human approval
The service owner approves the specific diff and target environment; production needs a second approver.
Execution boundary
The pipeline accepts only the approved artifact digest; credentials never reach the agent.
Record kept
Diff, test evidence, approvers, artifact digest, rollout result, and rollback path.
Finance and procurement

Payment or Procurement Workflow Agent

AI proposes
Matches an invoice to a purchase order and proposes a payment.
Constraints
Amount ceilings by role; payee on a verified list; segregation of duties; duplicate detection.
Human approval
The budget owner approves payee, amount, and account; amounts above a threshold also need finance control.
Execution boundary
The payment service releases only the bound payee and amount; changed bank details force a new approval.
Record kept
Documents matched, rule results, approvals, and the payment reference.
Security and IT

Identity and Permission Administration Agent

AI proposes
Proposes granting or removing access based on a request and the requester's role.
Constraints
Least privilege; time-boxed grants; no self-approval; additional review for sensitive roles.
Human approval
The resource owner approves the exact role, scope, and duration.
Execution boundary
The gateway applies the change with an expiring grant; a broader grant than approved is rejected.
Record kept
Request, justification, approver, grant start and expiry, and any revocation.
Manufacturing

Maintenance Decision Assistant

AI proposes
Proposes an inspection plan or a hold recommendation from sensor trends and failure history.
Constraints
Safety-critical equipment decisions are never resolved automatically; missing sensor data triggers escalation; the current maintenance policy version applies.
Human approval
A maintenance lead or safety officer approves any stop-or-continue decision on safety-critical equipment.
Execution boundary
Work orders and equipment commands go through a controlled interface with fail-safe defaults.
Record kept
Sensor data and its version, policy version, alternatives considered, approver, and outcome.
07 / Implementation Roadmap

Do Not Start With Full Autonomy

Start by making decisions visible, then bounded, then verifiable, then executable under control. Widen delegation only as evidence from each stage supports it.

Stages

From visible decisions to governed autonomy

  1. Stage 1 Evidence and traceability Capture sources, versions, and a structured Decision Trace for one workflow. Run the AI in proposal-only mode. see
  2. ↓
  3. Stage 2 Role, authority, and policy boundaries Define agent identities, scoped permissions, risk tiers, and policies a system can enforce. Treat unclassified cases as high risk by default. bound
  4. ↓
  5. Stage 3 Approval binding for consequential actions Freeze payloads and bind approvals with hash, scope, expiry, and single use. Begin with the few actions that are costly to reverse. verify
  6. ↓
  7. Stage 4 Controlled execution gateways Move credentials behind the gateway. Re-validate at execution, fail closed, and add prepare and commit for irreversible steps. control
  8. ↓
  9. Stage 5 Continuous review, simulation, and governance Sample decisions, watch for drift, rehearse revocation and emergency stop, and widen or narrow autonomy based on measured results. improve

Start Narrow

Choose one workflow that has real consequences but a contained blast radius. Complete Stages 1 to 3 there before replicating the pattern. The goal of the first release is not autonomy; it is a path in which every consequential step can be checked.

Autonomy Is Earned

Moving a case class from human-approved to automatic is an expansion of delegation, not a threshold tweak. It should follow evidence: calibrated evaluation, verified rollback and stop paths, and agreement from the business and risk owners.

Autonomy is granted by evidence, not by ambition.
Foundations

Foundations: Trust as a Bounded, Revisable Expectation

The architecture above rests on a specific view of trust, developed in the Chinoba practical guide. Trust is not a fixed score attached to a person or an AI. It is a bounded, revisable expectation — held by a specific evaluator, about a specific subject, for a specific purpose, role, situation, and time horizon. The sections below keep that foundation: why conventional trust evaluation falls short, how Decision Trace and Knowledge Flow feed a Trust Knowledge Graph, how a Trust Engine forms an expectation, and how Boundary and Human Authority govern execution.

08 / Why Conventional Trust Evaluation Is Not Enough

Why Conventional Trust Evaluation Is Not Enough

A reputation score, a past success rate, and a single trust number are useful for ranking and anomaly detection. They are not sufficient grounds for delegating a business role to an AI Agent and being accountable for the result, because aggregating the past into one value discards exactly the conditions needed to judge the present.

What Gets Lost in a Single Score

A quality-inspection Agent that judged 95 of the last 100 cases correctly has a 95% success rate. That number alone cannot say:

  • which products, lines, or equipment the 95 cases involved
  • whether the same sensors and quality policy are still in effect
  • whether the 5 misses were minor or safety-related
  • whether the model or prompt changed during that period

The same gap separates creditworthiness, reputation, and reliability from trust itself: each compresses past information into a signal, but only trust asks whether expected behavior will hold in this context, for this goal, right now.

Evidence, Risk, Confidence, and Trust Are Not the Same

A track record, a benchmark, or a certification is Evidence offered in support of trust — it is not trust itself, and it does not update automatically as context changes. Risk describes what is at stake if expectation fails; Confidence describes how certain an estimate is; Trust is the bounded expectation that combines them for a specific role and moment.

Collapsing factors of a different nature into one averaged value is the most dangerous mistake in trust design:

Capability 0.95, Reputation 0.92, Safety 0.20 Average = 0.69 — above a 0.65 threshold. A hazardous task cannot be justified this way.

Trust Does Not Substitute for Permission

High trust, however earned, does not replace Permission, and it does not authorize crossing a Boundary. Trust can widen what is offered for approval; it cannot dissolve the approval step or a limit that exists for other reasons.

Trust Is Relational, Not a Property of a Subject

"Agent A is trustworthy" omits the evaluator, the role, the goal, the context, and the time horizon — without them, nothing about what can actually be delegated can be determined. Trust exists in the combination:

Trust Context = Evaluator × Target × Role × Goal × Situation × Policy Set × Constraint Set × Time Horizon
09 / Decision Trace: Recording Decisions, Not Just Results

Decision Trace: Recording Decisions, Not Just Results

A decision cannot be understood from its result alone. A good outcome can be a lucky success; a bad outcome can follow a well-reasoned decision, correctly executed. Decision Trace is the starting point of Trust Infrastructure because it is the structured record that lets the two be told apart.

What a Trace Connects

A Decision Trace binds together, in sequence:

  • Observation and Context
  • Knowledge and Provenance
  • Alternatives considered
  • applied Policy and Constraint
  • the Decision itself
  • the Action taken
  • the Result
  • Feedback into knowledge, policy, and trust

It does not preserve a model's raw internal reasoning. It is a structured, reviewable record built for verification, explanation, and improvement after the fact — not a replay of what happened inside the model.

Do Not Conflate the Roles

An AI's proposal, a human's approval, modification, or rejection, the party who executes an action, and the party who bears final responsibility for it are distinct roles. Collapsing them into "the AI decided" or "the human is responsible" erases the exact information a trace exists to preserve.

A good result does not prove a good decision. A bad result does not prove a bad one. Without a trace, a lucky success and a principled, correctly executed failure stay indistinguishable.
10 / Knowledge Flow and the Trust Knowledge Graph

Knowledge Flow and the Trust Knowledge Graph

Knowledge Flow delivers the meaning, source, authority, validity period, scope, confidentiality, and update status of knowledge scattered across an organization to the moment a decision is made — not just the document text. A Trust Knowledge Graph is what lets that delivered knowledge become the grounds a trust judgment can be queried against.

More Than a Knowledge Graph

A Trust Knowledge Graph is heterogeneous by design, connecting node categories whose meaning and lifecycle differ:

  • Actor, Agent, Organization, Role
  • Goal, Constraint, Policy
  • Context, Situation, Event
  • Knowledge, Evidence, Source
  • Decision, Action, Result
  • Risk, Expectation, Confidence
  • Human Gate, Approval, Escalation

Entity, Event, and Assertion are kept as distinct node types. Overwriting "equipment M1 is at 82°C" as a property of the equipment node erases the past state and the source that claimed it — it must instead be an Event tied to the sensor, the source, and the timestamp.

Trust Context Is the Query Boundary

Trust inference does not walk the entire graph from an actor node outward. It cuts a subgraph starting from a Trust Context, containing at minimum:

  • Evaluator and Target
  • Role and Goal
  • Situation
  • Policy Set and Constraint Set
  • Time Horizon

The same Agent, evaluated for a different role, goal, policy version, counterparty, or time window, is a different Trust Context — past results can be reused, but a past evaluation value must never be copied over unconditionally.

Entity is not Event. Event is not Assertion. Assertion is not Evidence. Evidence is not Expectation. Confusing them corrupts the graph that trust is supposed to be queried from.

Because past decisions must be reconstructable, not only current knowledge, the graph tracks valid time, transaction time, version, and provenance for every node — so a Trust Context evaluated today can be re-derived, and a Trust Context evaluated a year ago can still be reconstructed as it stood at that moment.

11 / Trust Engine and Expectation Graph

Trust Engine and Expectation Graph

The Trust Engine is the runtime structure that turns Decision Trace and current Knowledge into an Expectation Graph — a structured account of what decision, action, and result can be expected in the current Trust Context, and why.

i

Context Analyzer

Turns an incoming decision request into a Trust Context — role, goal, situation, policy, and time horizon — rather than passing on a bare subject ID.

ii

Decision Trace Retriever

Retrieves comparable past traces by role, goal, policy version, risk, and outcome quality — not similarity alone, and not successes only.

iii

Knowledge Integrator

Fixes currently applicable policy, constraint, and evidence into a Knowledge Snapshot, and surfaces contradictions it cannot resolve rather than averaging over them.

iv

Expectation Graph Builder

Builds a temporary, versioned subgraph — evaluator, subject, role, situation, policy, similar traces, expected decision, action, result, and risk — for this one evaluation.

v

Expectation Reasoner

Performs symbolic reasoning over the graph, ranking absolute boundary above statute, above human authority, above policy, above constraint, above learned pattern.

vi

Trust Output Layer

Combines symbolic and numeric output with risk and boundary into one versioned result — allow, allow-with-monitoring, hold, deny, or human review — not a threshold on a single score.

vii

Execution Monitor

Observes governed execution and records actual behavior as it happens — decision, tool call, policy check, approval, timeout, and recovery — as its own evidence plane.

viii

Trust Evaluator

Compares expected against actual behavior across goal alignment, policy and constraint compliance, safety, consistency, and recovery — kept as a vector, not collapsed into one number.

ix

Learning & Feedback

Routes each evaluation to the trace, the knowledge graph, policy review, or training data by cause — not everything toward retraining a model.

Symbolic and Neural Are Complementary by Design

The Expectation Graph is a symbolic structure — an evidence path and a reasoning path a human can walk. A graph neural network can, by design, be layered over that same graph to estimate numeric quantities such as expected-behavior probability, risk score, or confidence. The two are complementary design choices in this architecture, not a claim that GNN-based trust inference is a deployed or production-proven capability.

A Number Is Not the Verdict

Any numeric output — trust score, risk score, confidence — is treated as reference information alongside uncertainty, impact, and an explanation path, not as a threshold that by itself authorizes execution.

A higher-ranked hard constraint is never overridden by a lower-ranked score. A 0.99 probability of success does not permit what policy requires a human to approve.
12 / Trust, Boundary, Human Authority, and Runtime Decision

Trust, Boundary, Human Authority, and Runtime Decision

Trust is reference information for a runtime decision — it is not, by itself, permission to execute. Whether an action proceeds is evaluated together with Policy, Permission, Risk, Boundary, and Human Authority, every time.

What Boundary Protects

Boundary marks the limits that no trust score and no efficiency gain may trade away — life and safety, human rights, personal data, critical assets, and social infrastructure. A hard boundary violation returns Reject regardless of how high a trust inference scores.

Human Gate as a Real Control Point

A Human Gate is not an approval button clicked from habit. It presents the evidence behind a recommendation, the candidate options, the assessed risk, the potential impact, and the deadline for a decision — and it accepts approve, reject, modify, or dispute, not only yes.

Low risk, cleared thresholds — act. Uncertain, but explainable — ask, with the graph attached. Boundary or trust exceeded — stop, hold, or quarantine.
13 / More Application Domains

More Application Domains

Beyond the five designs above, the same pattern applies across other domains. In each case, trust is not a score attached to a system. It is knowledge, context, and policy resolved into an expectation, checked against human authority, recorded as a decision trace, and corrected by outcome feedback. Higher-risk domains are described as design questions requiring governance, not as claims about deployed effect.

Manufacturing

Manufacturing Quality Decisions

A shipment-hold decision draws on the current quality policy and design documents, not just an anomaly score, and routes critical defects to a Human Gate before release.

Equipment

Maintenance and Shutdown Decisions

Stop-or-continue judgments weigh safety, productivity, and delivery against past failure traces and sensor data, escalating rather than resolving silently under competing pressure.

Finance

Financial Transactions and Risk

Approval, hold, and review decisions are explained through the trust graph behind them, with bias and fairness checked as part of the evaluation, not after the fact.

Healthcare — design question

Medical Support AI

A recommendation is only as good as the patient context and guidelines behind it; confidence, uncertainty, and a Human Gate to the physician are treated as required governance, not optional polish.

Legal

Contract Review and Legal Support

A clause is checked against current law and internal rules and past contract traces before it shapes a recommendation, with unresolved risk escalated to legal staff.

Multi-Agent

Multi-Agent Coordination

Deciding which agent to entrust with a role draws on an inter-agent trust graph, with limits on how far trust propagates and explicit detection of coordination failure.

Supply Chain

Cross-Organizational Coordination

Trust between companies is shared as verifiable, minimized evidence bound to a specific purpose and context — not a permanent score handed to a partner.

Public sector — design question

Public AI and Social Infrastructure

Government and public AI require explainability to citizens, fairness, scoped disclosure of the trust graph, and a working appeals process as governance requirements, not features.

14 / Operations, Evaluation, and Maturity

Operations, Evaluation, and Maturity

Trust Infrastructure is operated, not just designed: monitoring, alerting, fail-safes, rollback, security, privacy, and ethics apply to a Trust Engine the same way they apply to any other production system deciding real outcomes.

What Gets Measured

  • Predictive Accuracy — against a named target, not "trustworthy vs. not"
  • Calibration Error — whether a stated probability matches observed frequency
  • Explanation Fidelity — whether an explanation reflects the decision actually made
  • Graph Completeness — whether required evidence and policy are present, not graph size
  • Policy Compliance Rate — process compliance, not only good outcomes
  • Escalation Accuracy — escalation is expected behavior, not a failure to minimize
  • False Trust / False Distrust — the two errors are not symmetric in consequence
  • Robustness and Drift Detection — data, concept, policy, and graph drift, tracked separately

PoC to Production, Deliberately

A Trust Engine earns production scope the same way any governed system does: a narrow PoC on one business process, explicit human-in-the-loop before autonomy widens, and evaluation against the metrics above before the next stage of scope is granted — not a single launch decision.

Maturity is not automation rate. It is how much evidence, boundary, human authority, and improvement the system carries at each stage.
Maturity Model

From Logs Only to Social Trust Infrastructure

  1. L0 Logs Only Application and audit logs record what happened; why a particular decision was chosen is not yet structured. logs
  2. ↓
  3. L1 Decision Trace Execution events become structured traces — need, context, policy, alternatives, decision, action, result, feedback. trace
  4. ↓
  5. L2 Knowledge Integration Current policy, constraint, and design knowledge — versioned, sourced, and time-bound — connect to the trace. knowledge
  6. ↓
  7. L3 Expectation Graph Expected decision, action, result, alternatives, and risk are represented as a structure, not a single rule output. expectation
  8. ↓
  9. L4 Numeric Inference via GNN A graph neural network adds probability, risk, and confidence estimates alongside the symbolic structure — optional, not required to proceed. numeric
  10. ↓
  11. L5 Continuous Learning Expected vs. actual behavior feeds back into trace, knowledge, graph, and thresholds under governed, versioned release. feedback
  12. ↓
  13. L6 Cross-Organizational Trust Trust extends to suppliers, partners, and providers as verifiable, minimized, purpose-bound evidence — not a shared permanent score. cross-org
  14. ↓
  15. L7 Social Trust Infrastructure Trust connects to public institutions — government, health, finance, energy — as an appealable, correctable civic capability, not a centralized score. social
